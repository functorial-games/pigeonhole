## Full-recall summary

Vakil deliberately teaches a special case: first-quadrant double complexes.

Start with objects \(E^{p,q}\) and two anticommuting differentials, one horizontal and one vertical. The total differential is their sum.

A spectral sequence can start by taking homology in one direction. The surviving classes inherit a differential in the other direction. Taking homology again produces another page. Later differentials come from longer zig-zags through the double complex.

The crucial pedagogical move is that Vakil does not start by proving exotic theorems. He uses spectral sequences to re-prove familiar diagram-chasing results:

- the Snake Lemma;
- the Five Lemma;
- the long exact sequence associated to a short exact sequence of complexes;
- the mapping cone exact sequence.

This is valuable for the game because the player can first recognize a known exactness pattern and only then discover that the same motion is a spectral-sequence calculation.

Another central lesson is that a double complex has two natural orientations: row-first or column-first. Both are trying to compute information about the same total complex. Often one orientation is easy and forces conclusions about the other.

That “compute the same thing by two compatible routes” is the more structural game idea to preserve.
